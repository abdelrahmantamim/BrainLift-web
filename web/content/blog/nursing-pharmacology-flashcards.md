Nursing pharm feels impossible when you treat it as a list of 300 drugs. It gets manageable once you learn drug classes, because drugs in a class share most of what you need to know. This guide gives you a suffix table, a card template, and a semester schedule.

*For study only. Drug information here covers well-known basics and is not complete. Verify every card against your drug guide and your program's materials.*

## Learn classes and prototypes, not 300 drugs

Most drugs belong to a class. Drugs in a class tend to share a mechanism, major side effects, and nursing considerations.

So learn each class through one **prototype**, the drug your course uses as the example. Learn the prototype well. Then, when you meet a new drug, ask two questions:

1. Which class is it in?
2. How does it differ from the prototype, if at all?

That turns 300 separate facts into about 30 classes plus a few differences. It also matches how exam questions are often written: you may see a drug you don't recognize, but the suffix tells you the class.

## Suffix table: high-yield classes

These suffixes cover many classes nursing students meet early. The "key nursing points" column is a starting point, not a complete list.

| Suffix | Class | Example | Key nursing points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| -olol | Beta blockers | Metoprolol | Check apical pulse and BP before giving. Can mask signs of hypoglycemia |
| -pril | ACE inhibitors | Lisinopril | Dry cough. Watch for angioedema and high potassium |
| -sartan | Angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) | Losartan | Watch BP and potassium. Avoid in pregnancy |
| -dipine | Calcium channel blockers (dihydropyridine) | Amlodipine | Hypotension, headache, peripheral edema |
| -statin | HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors | Atorvastatin | Report unexplained muscle pain. Monitor liver function |
| -thiazide | Thiazide diuretics | Hydrochlorothiazide | Watch for low potassium |
| -parin | Heparins | Enoxaparin | Bleeding risk. Antidote is protamine sulfate |
| -prazole | Proton pump inhibitors | Omeprazole | Usually taken before a meal. Long-term use has risks to review |
| -tidine | H2 receptor blockers | Famotidine | Reduce stomach acid |
| -setron | 5-HT3 antagonists (antiemetics) | Ondansetron | Headache, constipation, QT prolongation risk |
| -pam, -lam | Benzodiazepines | Lorazepam | Sedation, respiratory depression. Antidote is flumazenil |
| -cillin | Penicillins | Amoxicillin | Ask about allergies. Watch for anaphylaxis |
| -floxacin | Fluoroquinolones | Ciprofloxacin | Tendon rupture risk |
| -cycline | Tetracyclines | Doxycycline | Photosensitivity. Not usually given to young children |
| -mycin, -micin | Aminoglycosides | Gentamicin | Ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity. Peak and trough levels |
| -azole | Azole antifungals | Fluconazole | Many drug interactions |
| -vir | Antivirals | Acyclovir | Hydration, kidney function |
| -sone, -olone | Corticosteroids | Prednisone | High blood glucose, infection risk. Don't stop abruptly |
| -dronate | Bisphosphonates | Alendronate | Take with a full glass of water on an empty stomach, stay upright |
| -gliflozin | SGLT2 inhibitors | Empagliflozin | Genital yeast infections, UTIs, dehydration |

### Watch the exceptions

Suffixes are shortcuts, not rules. A few traps:

- **-mycin is shared.** Macrolides like azithromycin also end in -mycin but are a different class from aminoglycosides.
- **-azole is shared.** Omeprazole ends in -azole but is a PPI, not an antifungal. Metronidazole is an antibiotic.
- **Some key drugs have no helpful suffix.** Warfarin, digoxin, and furosemide each need their own cards.

## The drug-card template

Use the same layout for every class. Consistency makes cards faster to write and easier to review.

**Front:** One question, such as "Metoprolol: class and key nursing checks?"

**Back:**

- **Class:** Beta blocker (-olol)
- **Used for:** Hypertension, angina, heart failure, some dysrhythmias
- **Key side effects:** Bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue
- **Nursing checks:** Apical pulse and BP before giving. Hold and notify per parameters
- **Teaching:** Don't stop suddenly. Change positions slowly

Then split it. One giant card is hard to grade honestly. Make several small cards from the same template:

- "Suffix -olol means which class?"
- "What do you check before giving metoprolol?"
- "Why shouldn't a client stop a beta blocker suddenly?"

Small cards tell you exactly which part you don't know.

## Mnemonics that actually help

Mnemonics are popular in nursing school, but be honest about what they do. A major review of study techniques rated the keyword mnemonic as **low** utility overall, and rated practice testing and spaced practice as high ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)).

That doesn't mean mnemonics are useless. It means they should support recall practice, not replace it. Use them like this:

- **Use suffixes as your main "mnemonic."** They're built into the drug name, so they work on drugs you've never seen.
- **Keep mnemonics for short, fixed lists.** A memory hook for a set of side effects can help.
- **Still quiz yourself.** A mnemonic you can recite but can't apply won't help on a scenario question.

## Turning your pharm lecture slides into cards

Your lecture slides already highlight the drugs and points your instructor cares about. That makes them the best source for your deck.

### The manual way

1. Go through each slide and list every drug mentioned.
2. Group drugs by class and mark the prototype.
3. Fill in the template for each prototype.
4. Split each template into three to five small cards.
5. Add a card for any drug your instructor stressed that breaks the class pattern.

This takes a while for a full lecture. It's also the step most students skip when exams pile up.

## A spaced review plan for the semester

Pharm builds week by week, and finals usually cover all of it. Short daily reviews beat long sessions before each exam.

| When | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| After each lecture | Make cards for that lecture's classes the same day or next day |
| Daily, 15 to 20 minutes | Review cards that are due, new ones first |
| Weekly | Mix in older classes so nothing goes untouched for weeks |
| After each quiz or exam | Turn every missed question into new cards |
| Two weeks before finals | Run mixed quizzes across all classes, not unit by unit |

If you're also preparing for the NCLEX, pharm cards become part of a larger plan. Read [how to study for the NCLEX with flashcards](/blog/nclex-flashcards-study-guide/) for a 6-week schedule.

## Build your free starter deck

You don't need to buy a deck. Build a starter deck from the suffix table above, for free, in an afternoon:

1. Make one card per row: "Suffix -pril means which class?"
2. Make one card per prototype: "Lisinopril: key nursing points?"
3. Add cards for the exceptions: warfarin, digoxin, furosemide, and the shared suffixes.
4. Check each card against your drug guide before you start reviewing.

## FAQ

### What is the fastest way to memorize drug classes?

Learn one prototype per class, use suffixes to sort new drugs into classes, and quiz yourself daily with flashcards. Recall practice spread over time works better than rereading drug lists.

### Do drug suffixes always tell you the class?

No. Most do, but some endings are shared, like -mycin and -azole, and some important drugs have no class suffix. Make separate cards for the exceptions.

### How many pharmacology flashcards do I need?

Start with one card per suffix and three to five per prototype. Add more as your course covers new drugs and as you miss questions.

### Are pharmacology mnemonics worth it?

They can help with short fixed lists, but research rates the keyword mnemonic as low utility compared with practice testing. Use them alongside self-quizzing, not instead of it.

### Can I trust AI-made pharmacology flashcards?

Treat them as a draft. Check every card against your drug guide or course materials, because AI can make mistakes and drug errors matter.
