Language classes test you on the words and grammar from your own textbook chapter, usually through translation, fill-in-the-blank, and short reading passages. You cannot reason your way to a word you never memorized. Vocabulary has to be reviewed again and again, at the right moments, until it is automatic.

## Where learning a language breaks down

### Typing out vocab lists is slow

Every chapter ends with a list of thirty or forty words, and copying them into a flashcard app by hand takes longer than learning a few of them. Take a picture of the vocabulary page instead. BrainLift reads the words and turns them into a deck and a quiz.

### New words vanish within days

You learn a word on Monday and it is gone by Friday unless you see it again. Spaced repetition brings each card back just as you are about to forget it. In BrainLift you rate how well you knew each word, and that rating decides when it returns.

### General courses do not follow your syllabus

A general language course teaches its own word lists in its own order. Your quiz on Thursday covers chapter four of your textbook. Building decks from your own pages, handouts, and class notes keeps your practice lined up with what your teacher will ask.

## What a generated quiz looks like for language learners

This example uses beginner Spanish from a chapter on food and daily life.

**1. At a restaurant, what are you asking for when you say "la cuenta, por favor"?**
A) The menu
B) The bill (correct)
C) A table
D) The tip
*Why:* "La cuenta" means the bill or the check in this context.

**2. "¿Dónde está la biblioteca?" means:**
A) Where is the bookstore?
B) Where is the library? (correct)
C) When does the library open?
D) Where is the bakery?
*Why:* "Biblioteca" is library; "librería" is the false friend that means bookstore.

**3. Choose the correct verb: "Yo ___ estudiante."**
A) estoy
B) soy (correct)
C) es
D) está
*Why:* Use "ser" for identity and occupation, and "soy" is its first-person form.

| Front | Back |
|---|---|
| el desayuno | breakfast |
| embarazada | pregnant (a false friend; it does not mean embarrassed) |
| tener hambre | to be hungry |
| la carta / el menú | the menu |

Usage changes from region to region, so check meanings and spelling with your textbook or teacher.

## How it works

1. Photograph a vocabulary list, a dialogue, or even a real menu or sign.
2. Choose a difficulty and the number of questions, then quiz yourself on meanings and usage.
3. Review the deck a few minutes each day, rating each word, and keep your streak going.

## Study habits that work for language learners

Review a little every day rather than a lot once a week. Vocabulary suits this well, because each word is a small, separate fact you can review in seconds. A synthesis of hundreds of experiments on spaced practice found better recall when sessions were spread out, and the ideal gap depended on how long you need to remember ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)).

Practice both directions. Recognizing "la cuenta" is easier than producing it when you need it, so include cards that start from English too. ## FAQ

### Which languages does it support?

It works from whatever text you photograph, upload, or type. Results can vary by language and handwriting, so start with a small deck and check the cards before relying on them.

### Does it replace a language course app?

No. Use it alongside your class or course to drill the specific words and grammar you are being tested on.

### Can I share a vocab deck with my class?

Yes. Share any deck with classmates through a link or a QR code so everyone studies the same list.
