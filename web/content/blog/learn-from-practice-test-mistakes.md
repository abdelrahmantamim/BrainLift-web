You finish a practice test, check the score, feel good or bad about it, and move on. That habit throws away the most useful part of the test. The questions you missed are a personalized study guide, and this post shows you how to use it.

## Why reviewing only your score wastes the test

A score tells you how you did. It doesn't tell you what to do next. Two students can both score 72% and need completely different fixes.

Taking the test already helped you. Retrieval practice, pulling an answer out of memory, beats rereading for long-term retention. In one classic study, students who took a practice test remembered more after two days and after one week than students who restudied the same passage ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)).

But a test can only strengthen what you managed to recall. The questions you got wrong stay wrong unless you do something with them. That's where an error log comes in.

## The 4 kinds of mistakes

Before you fix a miss, name it. Most wrong answers fall into one of four buckets.

| Type | What it looks like | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge gap | You didn't know the fact or concept | Learn it, then make a flashcard |
| Misread | You knew it but missed "NOT," "first," or "except" | Slow down on the stem, circle key words |
| Careless | Arithmetic slip, bubbled the wrong letter | Build a quick check step into your routine |
| Timing | You rushed or guessed because the clock ran out | Practice timed sets, flag and skip hard items |

Be honest here. It's tempting to label everything "careless" because it feels less bad. If you couldn't explain why the right answer is right, it's a knowledge gap.

Only knowledge gaps need new flashcards. The other three are habit problems. You fix those by practicing differently, not by memorizing more.

## Building an error log

An error log is a simple table you add to after every practice test. A spreadsheet or a notebook page both work. Use these five columns.

1. **Question.** Write a short version, enough to recognize it later.
2. **My answer.** What you picked and, briefly, why.
3. **Why it was wrong.** One of the four types, plus one sentence.
4. **The rule.** The fact, formula, or principle that gets it right next time.
5. **Card.** A check mark once you've turned the rule into a flashcard.

Keep the "rule" column short. "Loop diuretics can drop potassium" is a rule. A copied paragraph from the answer explanation is not.

After two or three tests, read down the "why it was wrong" column. Patterns show up fast. Maybe half your misses are misreads on "except" questions. That's a five-minute fix worth points on every future test.

## Turning each miss into a flashcard

The rule column is your card front and back. Write the front as a question you could be asked, and keep one idea per card.

Here's how a miss becomes a card:

- **Miss:** You picked "increases Vmax" for a competitive inhibitor.
- **Rule:** Competitive inhibitors raise apparent Km; Vmax stays the same.
- **Card front:** "A competitive inhibitor changes which kinetic value, Km or Vmax?"
- **Card back:** "Apparent Km goes up. Vmax is unchanged because enough substrate can outcompete the inhibitor."

Notice the back explains why. That makes the card harder to memorize by shape alone. You have to actually understand it.

Avoid copying the original question word for word. If you only see the exact same wording, you learn to recognize that question, not the concept. Rephrase it, or write two cards that come at the idea from different angles.

## Re-testing missed items on a spaced schedule

One review won't stick. Missed items need to come back a few times, with growing gaps between them.

A simple schedule that works on paper:

- **Day 1:** Review the new cards right after the test.
- **Day 3:** Review again. Anything you miss goes back to day 1.
- **Day 7:** Review again.
- **Day 14 and beyond:** Review cards you still hesitate on.

A card "graduates" when you can answer it quickly and correctly twice in a row, days apart. Until then, it stays in rotation.

If you want the theory behind why spacing and testing work together, see [active recall vs spaced repetition](/blog/active-recall-vs-spaced-repetition/). Practice testing and distributed practice are the two techniques a major review of study methods rated highest in usefulness ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)).

## Examples: NCLEX, MCAT, AP Chem

Here's what one row of the error log looks like in three different exams.

### NCLEX

- **Question:** Client on furosemide reports leg cramps and weakness. Which lab do you check first?
- **My answer:** Sodium.
- **Why wrong:** Knowledge gap. I mixed up which electrolyte loop diuretics affect most.
- **Rule:** Loop diuretics like furosemide cause potassium loss. Cramps and weakness suggest hypokalemia.
- **Card:** "Furosemide plus muscle cramps: which electrolyte do you suspect?" / "Low potassium (hypokalemia)."

For a full NCLEX plan built around this loop, read [how to study for the NCLEX with flashcards](/blog/nclex-flashcards-study-guide/).

### MCAT

- **Question:** Effect of a competitive inhibitor on a Lineweaver-Burk plot.
- **My answer:** Changed y-intercept.
- **Why wrong:** Knowledge gap. Mixed up competitive and noncompetitive.
- **Rule:** Competitive inhibition changes the x-intercept (Km) and keeps the y-intercept (1/Vmax) the same.
- **Card:** "On a Lineweaver-Burk plot, which intercept stays fixed with a competitive inhibitor?" / "The y-intercept, because Vmax doesn't change."

MCAT students weighing flashcard tools can compare options in [MCAT Anki alternatives](/blog/mcat-anki-alternatives/).

### AP Chemistry

- **Question:** Find the limiting reactant in a reaction given masses of both reactants.
- **My answer:** The reactant with the smaller mass.
- **Why wrong:** Knowledge gap. I compared grams instead of moles.
- **Rule:** Convert both to moles, divide by each coefficient, and the smaller result is limiting.
- **Card:** "How do you find the limiting reactant?" / "Convert to moles, divide by the coefficient, smallest value is limiting. Never compare grams directly."

These sample cards cover standard textbook facts, but check them against your course materials, since wording and emphasis vary by class.

## FAQ

### How long should I spend reviewing a practice test?

Plan to spend about as long reviewing as you spent taking it. Go through every miss and every lucky guess, since a guess you got right is still a gap.

### Should I review questions I got right?

Review the ones you guessed on or weren't sure about. Treat those like misses and log them. Skip the ones you knew cold.

### What is a wrong answer journal?

It's another name for an error log: a running record of each missed question, why you missed it, and the rule that fixes it. The format matters less than doing it after every test.

### How many practice tests should I take before the exam?

There's no magic number. Take one, review it fully, study your error log for a few days, then take the next. Taking tests faster than you can review them wastes the most valuable part.

### Can I make an error log from a quiz my friend shared?

Yes. Any set of questions works, as long as you log why you missed each one and write the rule in your own words.
