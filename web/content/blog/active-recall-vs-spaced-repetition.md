Active recall and spaced repetition get mentioned together so often that they blur into one idea. They're not the same thing, and they're not competitors either. Here's what each one is, what the research actually found, and how to use both for your next exam.

## The one-sentence difference

**Active recall is how you study. Spaced repetition is when you study.**

Active recall means pulling information out of memory, by answering a question, filling a blank, or explaining a concept without looking. Spaced repetition means spreading those study sessions out over days or weeks instead of packing them into one night.

You can do one without the other. Quizzing yourself 50 times in one evening is active recall without spacing. Rereading your notes once a week is spacing without active recall. The strongest approach combines both.

| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Testing yourself from memory | Spreading reviews out over time |
| Answers the question | How should I study? | When should I study? |
| Research name | Testing effect, retrieval practice | Spacing effect, distributed practice |

## Active recall: the testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke 2006)

The testing effect is the finding that retrieving information strengthens memory more than reviewing it again.

In a well-known experiment, students read short prose passages. Some then reread the passage, and others took a recall test on it. On a test five minutes later, the rereading group did better. On tests two days and one week later, the group that had practiced recall did better ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)).

That pattern explains why rereading feels effective. It helps in the short term, right when you're judging how well it's working. The benefit of testing shows up later, on the day that counts.

A later study compared retrieval practice with building concept maps, a method that feels much more active than rereading. Retrieval practice still produced more learning ([Karpicke & Blunt, 2011](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/research/2011/110120KarpickeScience.html)).

### What counts as active recall

- Answering practice questions without notes
- Flashcards where you say the answer before flipping
- Writing everything you remember about a topic on a blank page
- Explaining a concept out loud, then checking what you missed

If the answer is in front of you while you "study," it isn't active recall.

## Spaced repetition: the spacing effect (Cepeda et al. 2006)

The spacing effect is the finding that spreading study sessions out leads to better long-term memory than massing them together.

A large review pulled together 839 assessments from 317 experiments on spaced study. A key result: the best gap between study sessions gets longer as the time until the test gets longer ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). If your exam is next week, gaps of a day or two make sense. If you need to remember something for months, your gaps should stretch to weeks.

That's the logic behind spaced repetition apps. Each time you get a card right, the next review gets pushed further out. Each time you miss it, it comes back sooner.

## What a 10-technique review rated highest (Dunlosky 2013)

In 2013, a team of researchers reviewed ten common study techniques and rated how useful each one was, based on the evidence.

Two techniques got the top rating of high utility: practice testing and distributed practice. That's active recall and spaced repetition. Rereading, highlighting, and summarizing were rated low utility ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)).

Low utility doesn't mean useless. Reading the chapter once is how you learn what's in it. But once you've read it, more rereading is a poor use of your time compared with testing yourself.

## Using both together: a schedule for an exam N days away

Here's a simple plan that combines the two. Start with a self-quiz on everything, then review on gaps that grow toward the exam.

| Days until exam | Suggested review days (counting from today) |
| --- | --- |
| 7 | Day 1, 2, 4, 6 |
| 14 | Day 1, 2, 5, 9, 13 |
| 30 | Day 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, 28 |
| 60+ | Day 1, 4, 10, 20, then every 2 to 3 weeks |

These are starting points, not exact numbers from the research. The principle is what matters: short gaps at first, longer gaps later, and a final review a day or two before the exam.

### How to run each session

1. **Quiz yourself first.** Don't reread before testing. Let the test show you what's missing.
2. **Check every answer.** Feedback matters, especially for the ones you got wrong.
3. **Collect your misses.** Put wrong answers into their own pile or deck.
4. **Start the next session with the misses.** Then mix in material you got right last time.

Step 3 is where a lot of students lose time. Turning wrong answers into a new deck by hand is tedious, so it often doesn't happen. We cover the full method in [how to learn from your practice test mistakes](/blog/learn-from-practice-test-mistakes/).

## Where they fall short (understanding vs remembering)

Active recall and spacing are tools for remembering. They don't replace understanding the material in the first place.

- **You can't retrieve what you never understood.** If a concept confuses you, a flashcard about it will just help you memorize a sentence you don't get. Learn it first, with your textbook, lecture, or a tutor, then test it.
- **Card quality matters.** Cards that only ask for isolated facts won't prepare you for exam questions that ask you to apply ideas. Mix in practice problems and "why" questions. See [how to make flashcards from lecture notes](/blog/flashcards-from-lecture-notes/) for examples.
- **Lab studies are simplified.** Many experiments use word lists or short passages, not full courses. The results hold up across many settings, but your own course will be messier.
- **Feeling is a bad guide.** Rereading and cramming often feel more effective than they are. Trust your quiz scores over your sense of how it went.

## Do flashcard apps actually raise scores? (Anki and Step 1 studies)

Medical students have studied this question more than anyone, mostly using Anki and the USMLE Step 1 exam.

One study found that Anki use was associated with higher Step 1 scores ([Lu et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8651966)). But the data was observational and self-reported. Students who choose to use flashcards daily may also differ in other ways, such as how much they study overall.

After Step 1 moved to pass/fail scoring, follow-up studies showed mixed results ([2023 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10176558)).

So the honest answer is this. The underlying techniques, practice testing and spacing, have strong experimental support. Evidence that any specific app causes higher scores is weaker and mostly correlational. The app is a way to make the techniques easy to stick with. ## How to start tonight

You don't need a perfect system to begin. Do this:

1. **Pick one upcoming exam.** Count the days until it.
2. **Take one set of notes or one chapter.** Write or generate 15 to 20 questions from it.
3. **Answer them without looking.** Mark every miss.
4. **Check your answers against the source.** If the questions came from AI, confirm they match your notes, since generated questions can be wrong.
5. **Put review dates in your calendar.** Use the table above for your exam date.
6. **Next session, start with your misses.**

Twenty minutes tonight with a quiz will do more for the exam than an hour of rereading.

## FAQ

### Is active recall better than spaced repetition?

Neither is better, because they answer different questions. Active recall is the method of testing yourself, and spaced repetition is the timing of those tests. Using both together works best.

### Is active recall better than rereading?

For long-term memory, yes. In Roediger and Karpicke's study, rereading did better on a test five minutes later, but recall practice did better after two days and one week.

### What's a good spaced repetition schedule for an exam?

Start with short gaps of a day or two, then lengthen them as you go, with a final review a day or two before the exam. The further away the exam, the longer your gaps can be.

### Do I need an app for spaced repetition?

No. Paper flashcards and a calendar work. Apps make it easier by tracking which cards are due and scheduling hard cards sooner.

### Can I use active recall for subjects like math?

Yes. Redo worked problems without looking at the solution, and explain each step from memory. That's retrieval practice for procedures.
