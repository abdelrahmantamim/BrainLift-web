AP exams feel far away in January. Then spring break ends, a few units are still left in class, and suddenly you have three exams in one week. This guide shows you how to build a study plan from the notes you already have, starting in January, so May feels like a review instead of a rescue.

The plan works for any AP subject. History, biology, calculus, a language, or a mix. You adjust the content, not the structure.

## When are the 2027 AP exams?

The 2027 AP exams run over two weeks: **May 3 to 7** and **May 10 to 14, 2027** ([College Board AP Central](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/exam-administration-ordering-scores/exam-dates)). Each subject has its own date and time slot, so check the full schedule for every exam you are taking.

Write each of your exam dates in your calendar now. Then count backward. If your first exam is May 4 and your last is May 13, your plan has to peak twice, not once.

## Why starting in January works better than starting in April

Two ideas from learning research shape this plan.

First, practice testing and spaced practice are among the most useful study techniques, while rereading and highlighting are among the least useful ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). So the plan is built around answering questions, not reading notes.

Second, the longer you need to remember something, the wider the gaps between reviews should be ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). A January start lets you review a September unit a few times, weeks apart, before May. An April start squeezes all of that into days.

Starting early does not mean studying more each week. It means small, regular sessions that add up.

## Step 1: gather your raw material

Before you plan any weeks, collect what you already have for each AP class.

- **Unit notes.** Handwritten, typed, or photos of the board. Sort them by unit.
- **Unit tests and quizzes from class.** Your wrong answers here are gold.
- **The course and exam description.** It lists the units and skills the exam covers. Use it to spot gaps in your notes.
- **Past free-response questions and scoring guidelines.** College Board posts these on AP Central for most subjects. They show how points are actually earned.

Make one folder or notebook section per unit. If a unit has no notes, mark it now so you can fill the gap early instead of in May.

## Step 2: turn unit notes into practice questions

Each unit should become a set of questions you can answer from memory. Aim for 20 to 40 per unit, depending on how dense it is.

Good AP questions come in three types:

1. **Recall.** "What were the main causes of the event?" or "What does this enzyme do?"
2. **Explain.** "Why does this happen?" or "How does this cause that?"
3. **Apply.** "Given this graph, source, or data, what can you conclude?"

Recall questions are easy to write. Explain and apply questions are closer to what the exam rewards, so make sure every unit has some of each.

You can write these by hand. You can also photograph or upload your unit notes into an app and have it draft a quiz and a flashcard deck. Either way, check each answer against your notes and textbook, because AI-written questions can contain mistakes.

## Step 3: turn free-response rubrics into questions

Free-response rubrics tell you exactly what earns a point. That makes them excellent question material.

Take one past free-response question and its scoring guidelines. For each point on the rubric, write a question that targets it.

For example, if a rubric awards a point for "describes a cause and explains how it led to the outcome," write:

- **Front:** "What earns the causation point on this type of question?"
- **Back:** "Name a specific cause and explain the link to the outcome, not just list it."

If the rubric gives a point for a specific concept or calculation step, make a card for that concept or step.

Then do the full question under time, grade it with the rubric, and note every point you missed. Each missed point becomes a new question. Over a few months, you build a deck of exactly the reasoning moves the exam scores.

## Step 4: the week-by-week plan, January to May

The plan has four phases. The table gives the overview, and the sections below explain each one.

| Weeks | Dates (approx.) | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 4 | January | Build question sets for finished units |
| 5 to 9 | February to early March | Spaced review plus new units as you learn them |
| 10 to 14 | Mid March to mid April | Mixed review and free-response practice |
| 15 to 18 | Mid April to the exams | Timed practice and mistakes only |

Adjust the dates to your school calendar and your earliest exam.

### Weeks 1 to 4: build your question sets

- **Goal:** one question set for every unit you have already finished.
- **Each week:** convert two or three units into questions, then answer each new set once.
- **Time:** about 20 to 30 minutes per AP class, three or four days a week.

Do not try to master the units yet. You are building the tool you will use for the next four months.

### Weeks 5 to 9: spaced review plus new units

- **Goal:** keep old units fresh while adding new ones.
- **Each session:** a short mix of due flashcards from older units, then questions from the current unit.
- **After each class test:** add your wrong answers to your question set.

Rate your cards honestly as you review. Cards you miss should come back sooner. Cards you know well can wait longer. A spaced repetition app with self-rating handles that timing for you.

### Weeks 10 to 14: mixed review and free response

- **Goal:** practice switching between units, the way the exam does.
- **Each week:** two mixed quizzes per subject that pull from several units, plus one free-response question graded with the rubric.
- **Keep a mistakes list.** Write down every wrong answer and every missed rubric point.

Mixing units feels harder than reviewing one unit at a time. That is normal, and it is closer to the real exam.

### Weeks 15 to 18: timed practice and your mistakes

- **Goal:** sharpen, not learn new material.
- **Each week:** at least one timed practice section per subject.
- **Daily:** 15 to 20 minutes on your mistakes deck only.

In the last few days before each exam, review your missed questions and rubric points. Sleep matters more than one extra hour of cramming.

## Step 5: make mistakes the center of your review

Your wrong answers are the most useful data you have. They show exactly where your understanding breaks.

After every quiz, practice section, or graded free response:

1. Write the correct answer in your own words.
2. Note why you missed it. Did you forget a fact, misread the question, or skip a step?
3. Turn it into a flashcard and review it on a spaced schedule.

In a classic experiment, students who practiced recalling material beat students who reread it on tests two days and one week later ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)). Reviewing your mistakes as questions, not as a list you reread, puts that to work.

## Juggling several AP exams at once

Most AP students take more than one exam. A few rules keep the plan manageable.

- **Rotate subjects by day.** For example, two subjects on Monday and Wednesday, two others on Tuesday and Thursday.
- **Do flashcards daily, deep work rotated.** Short reviews for every subject each day, longer free-response practice on rotation.
- **Front-load the earliest exam.** In late April, shift extra time toward whichever exam comes first.
- **Protect one rest day a week.** A plan you can keep beats a perfect plan you abandon in March.

## FAQ

### When should I start studying for AP exams in May 2027?

January is a good starting point. It gives you about four months to build question sets, space your reviews, and practice under time before the exams on May 3 to 7 and May 10 to 14, 2027.

### How many hours a week should I study for each AP exam?

There is no single right number. A sustainable starting point is two or three short sessions per subject each week from January, rising in April as you add timed practice.

### Can I use free-response scoring guidelines to study?

Yes. Turn each rubric point into a question about what earns it, then answer past free-response questions under time and grade yourself with the guidelines.

### What if my class has not finished all the units before the exam?

Build question sets for each unit as you finish it in class. Use the course and exam description to preview any unit your class reaches late, so it is not brand new in May.

### Are AI-generated practice questions accurate enough for AP review?

They are a fast starting point, but they can contain mistakes. Check every answer against your notes, textbook, and the official course materials before you rely on it.
