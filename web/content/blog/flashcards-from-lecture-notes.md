Most flashcard decks fail before the first review. The cards are too long, too vague, or so easy you could answer them half asleep. This guide gives you a simple system for turning lecture notes into cards that make you think, plus a worked example from a biology lecture.

## What belongs on a flashcard (and what doesn't)

A flashcard is for one thing you need to pull out of memory on demand. That covers more than definitions.

**Good flashcard material:**
- Key terms and their meaning
- Cause and effect ("What triggers X?")
- Steps in a process, one step or link per card
- Comparisons ("How does X differ from Y?")
- Numbers, names, and formulas you must know cold

**Leave it out:**
- Examples your professor used once to tell a story
- Material you already know well
- Long explanations that only make sense as a paragraph
- Anything you can't phrase as a clear question

For big ideas that need reasoning, such as "explain why the experiment failed," a practice question or a short written answer works better than a card. More on that below.

## 6 rules for cards you can actually recall

1. **One fact per card.** If the answer has a list of five things, split it, or ask for one item at a time.
2. **Make the front a real question.** "Glycolysis" on the front is a prompt to recite everything. "Where in the cell does glycolysis happen?" has one clear answer.
3. **Force recall, not recognition.** If the answer is obvious from the question's wording, rewrite it.
4. **Use cloze deletions for fixed phrases.** "The ___ is the site of the electron transport chain" works well for exact facts.
5. **Write answers in your own words.** Short and plain beats a copied textbook sentence.
6. **Add context when two cards look alike.** Start with a short tag like "Photosynthesis:" so you don't confuse similar processes.

### Cloze or question and answer?

Use cloze for exact wording, like definitions and formulas. Use question and answer for anything that asks "why" or "how," because it makes you build the answer instead of filling one gap.

## Example: one lecture page to 12 good cards

Say your notes for one page of an intro biology lecture on cellular respiration read like this:

> Cellular respiration: glucose broken down to make ATP. 3 main stages: glycolysis (cytoplasm, glucose to 2 pyruvate, small ATP gain), Krebs/citric acid cycle (mitochondrial matrix), electron transport chain (inner mitochondrial membrane, makes most ATP). O2 = final electron acceptor. No O2: fermentation.

A weak card from this page puts "Cellular respiration" on the front and the whole paragraph on the back. You'll flip it, nod, and learn nothing.

Here are 12 better cards from the same page:

1. What is the main purpose of cellular respiration? *(To break down glucose and make ATP.)*
2. What are the three main stages of cellular respiration, in order?
3. Where in the cell does glycolysis take place? *(Cytoplasm.)*
4. What does glycolysis turn one glucose molecule into? *(Two pyruvate molecules.)*
5. Where does the Krebs cycle take place? *(Mitochondrial matrix.)*
6. What is another name for the Krebs cycle? *(Citric acid cycle.)*
7. Where is the electron transport chain located? *(Inner mitochondrial membrane.)*
8. Which stage produces most of the ATP?
9. What is the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain? *(Oxygen.)*
10. What happens to pyruvate when no oxygen is available? *(Fermentation.)*
11. Cloze: Glycolysis does not need ___ to run. *(Oxygen.)*
12. Why does the cell stop making most of its ATP when oxygen runs out?

Card 12 is the only one that asks you to connect ideas. Aim for a few of those per lecture.

## When a quiz beats a flashcard

Flashcards are great for facts. They're weaker for applying those facts to a new situation, which is what many exams test.

| Use a flashcard when | Use a practice question when |
| --- | --- |
| The answer is a term, name, or number | The answer needs reasoning |
| You need fast, repeated review | You need to apply a concept to a new case |
| The fact is stable and exact | The exam uses scenarios or problems |
| You're reviewing daily in short bursts | You're checking readiness before a test |

The best setup uses both. Take a quiz first to find gaps, then drill those gaps with cards. If your notes come from a textbook chapter rather than a lecture, see [how to turn a textbook PDF into practice questions](/blog/textbook-pdf-to-practice-questions/).

## Using AI to draft cards: a 5-point quality check

Writing 12 cards per page by hand takes a while. AI can draft them in seconds. You can paste typed notes, upload the PDF, or snap a photo in BrainLift, and it builds a quiz and a flashcard deck from the same material. If your notes are on paper, the guide to [studying from photos of your notes](/blog/study-from-photos-of-notes/) covers how to shoot them so they read cleanly.

AI drafts can still be wrong or sloppy. Run every new deck through this check:

1. **Is each answer in your notes?** Spot-check five cards against the source. Delete anything your class didn't cover.
2. **Is it one fact per card?** Split any card with a long list on the back.
3. **Does the question give away the answer?** Rewrite recognition-only cards.
4. **Are numbers, names, and units right?** These are the most common misreads.
5. **Would your professor phrase it this way?** If a card uses a different term than your course, change it to match.

This check takes about five minutes per lecture. If you'd rather start from a draft than a blank page, [BrainLift](/auth/register) turns pasted notes or a PDF into a deck you can edit.

## How often to review a new deck

Reviewing a deck once the night before the test gives you little lasting benefit. Spreading reviews out works far better.

A large review of spacing research found that the best gap between study sessions grows with how long you need to remember the material ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). For a test in a week, short gaps work. For a final in two months, the gaps should stretch out.

A simple starting schedule for a new deck:

- **Day 1:** Go through every card.
- **Day 2:** Review again, focusing on misses.
- **Day 4 or 5:** Review again.
- **Day 10 or so:** Review again.
- **Then:** Every couple of weeks until the exam.

If you rate each card as hard, okay, or easy after you flip it, a spaced repetition app can handle this schedule for you. For the research behind it, read [active recall vs spaced repetition](/blog/active-recall-vs-spaced-repetition/).

## Common mistakes: overloaded cards, recognition-only cards, too many decks

**Overloaded cards.** If you can't answer a card in a few seconds, it's too big. Split it.

**Recognition-only cards.** "Is the mitochondria the powerhouse of the cell? Yes or no" teaches you nothing. Ask for the answer, don't hand it over.

**Too many tiny decks.** It feels organized to keep one deck per lecture and study them one at a time. In one flashcard study, students did better with a single larger deck studied with spacing than with several small decks crammed one after another, and many students misjudged which method worked better ([Kornell, 2009](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994055985152)). Combine lecture decks into one course deck once you've made them.

**Making cards and never reviewing them.** The making is not the studying. Schedule the first review the same day you create the deck.

**Copying a classmate's deck without checking it.** Shared decks save time, but they may cover a different course or contain errors. Skim and edit before you rely on one. If you're weighing tools for shared decks, here's how [BrainLift compares with Quizlet](/compare/brainlift-vs-quizlet/).

## FAQ

### How many flashcards should I make per lecture?

Roughly 20 to 40 for a typical one-hour lecture, depending on how dense it is. Quality matters more than count, so cut cards for material you already know.

### Should I make flashcards during the lecture or after?

After. Take normal notes in class, then make cards the same day while the lecture is fresh. Making cards later is also a first round of review.

### Are AI-generated flashcards accurate?

Usually close, but not always. Check a sample against your notes, fix misread names and numbers, and delete anything your course didn't cover.

### Is it better to use cloze deletion or question and answer cards?

Use cloze for exact facts like definitions and formulas. Use question and answer for anything that asks how or why, since it makes you build the answer yourself.

### Should I keep one deck per lecture or one per course?

Make cards per lecture, then merge them into one course deck for review. Studying a mixed, larger deck with spacing tends to work better than cycling through small decks.
