You took the photos. The whiteboard, the professor's slide, the page of notes you scribbled in a rush. Now the exam is a week away and your camera roll is full of material you have not touched since. This guide shows how to turn those photos into questions you can actually practice, by hand or with an app.

## Why a camera roll full of notes doesn't help you remember

Looking at a photo of your notes is the same as rereading them. It feels productive because the material looks familiar. Familiar is not the same as remembered.

A large review of ten common study techniques rated rereading, highlighting, and summarizing as low utility, and rated practice testing and spaced practice as high utility ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). Photos sit firmly in the first group until you do something with them.

The fix is simple. Every photo should become a set of questions you answer from memory. The rest of this guide is about doing that without wasting an evening.

## How to photograph notes so AI (and you) can read them

Bad photos create bad questions. Whether you use an AI tool or just read them yourself later, a few habits make a big difference.

- **Shoot one page per photo.** Two pages in one shot means smaller text and more reading errors.
- **Get the phone flat above the page.** An angled shot bends the lines and squashes words at the far edge.
- **Use even light.** Daylight from a window works well. Avoid your own shadow and the glare from overhead lights on glossy paper.
- **Fill the frame.** Crop out the desk, your coffee, and the next page.
- **Write darker when you can.** Faint pencil is the hardest thing for text recognition to read.
- **For whiteboards, stand straight in front.** Take a second close-up of any small writing in the corners.

### Organize photos by lecture

Make an album for each class, then name or date each photo set by lecture. When exam week comes, you want "Bio, Lecture 7" in one place, not 300 photos sorted by the day you took them.

## Turning photos into questions: the manual way

You don't need any special tool for this. It takes longer, but it works.

1. **Open the photo next to a blank note.** Use your phone's notes app or a paper notebook.
2. **Read one section, then cover it.** A section is a heading, a diagram, or a block of about five lines.
3. **Write two or three questions about that section.** Ask "what," "why," and "how does this connect to" questions. Avoid questions you can answer by recognizing a word.
4. **Write the answer underneath, in your own words.** Then check it against the photo.
5. **Repeat for each section.** A typical page gives you six to ten questions.

You can also paste the text into a general chatbot like ChatGPT. Ask it to "write 10 short-answer questions from these notes, with answers, and quote the line each answer came from." The quote request makes checking much faster.

The weak point of the manual way is time. Most people make cards for the first lecture and give up by the third. If you'd rather skip the retyping, [BrainLift](/auth/register) builds a quiz and a flashcard deck straight from the photo.

## Make the cards stick: spacing your reviews

Making the questions is step one. Answering them more than once, with gaps in between, is what moves them into long-term memory.

In a classic experiment, students who reread a passage did better on a test five minutes later. Students who practiced recalling it did better two days and one week later ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)). Exams are rarely five minutes away, so retrieval wins.

A simple schedule for a new set of questions:

| When | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Same day | Answer every question once |
| Next day | Answer them again, mark the misses |
| 3 days later | Answer only the misses and a few you got right |
| 1 week later | Full set again |
| Before the exam | Misses only |

If you study in BrainLift, rate each card after you flip it. The app uses your rating to decide when that card comes back, so hard cards show up sooner and easy ones later. Read more about how the two ideas fit together in [active recall vs spaced repetition](/blog/active-recall-vs-spaced-repetition/).

## Checking AI-generated questions for accuracy

AI can misread a word, merge two ideas, or state something your notes never said. That's why you should check its output against the source before you trust it.

Use this quick check on every new set:

1. **Spot-check five answers against the photo.** Find the exact line each answer came from.
2. **Look for misread words.** Drug names, chemical formulas, dates, and numbers are the usual suspects.
3. **Delete anything not in your notes.** If a question covers material your class didn't teach, remove it.
4. **Fix vague questions.** "What is important about mitosis?" should become "What happens to chromosomes during anaphase?"
5. **Trust your professor over the app.** If your notes and the question disagree, your notes and the course materials win.

This takes a few minutes per lecture. It also counts as studying, because you're reading each answer with a question in mind.

## Handwriting, diagrams, and whiteboards: what works and what doesn't

Not every photo converts equally well. Here's what to expect.

**Works well:**
- Neat handwriting in pen
- Printed handouts and slides
- Whiteboards photographed straight on with good contrast
- Lists, definitions, and step-by-step processes

**Works with some fixing:**
- Messy or slanted handwriting. Expect a few misread words.
- Notes with arrows and margin comments. The connections may get lost.
- Pages with abbreviations only you understand. Spell out the key ones before you snap.

**Works poorly:**
- Faint pencil or photos in dim light
- Complex diagrams, such as anatomy labels or circuit drawings, where the meaning is in the picture rather than the text
- Math with lots of stacked fractions or symbols

For diagrams, make your own questions by hand. Cover the labels and ask yourself to name each part. That's still retrieval practice, and it's often faster than fixing an AI's attempt.

## FAQ

### Can AI read handwritten notes?

Yes, in most cases, if the handwriting is reasonably neat and the photo is sharp and well lit. Faint pencil, heavy slanting, and cramped margins cause more errors, so always check the generated questions against the original.

### How many questions should one page of notes give me?

Six to ten good questions per page is a useful target. More than that usually means some questions are too small or overlap.

### Is it better to make flashcards or a quiz from my photos?

Use both. A quiz shows you what you don't know yet, and flashcards are better for repeated review of those gaps over the following days.

### Should I still rewrite my notes?

Only if rewriting helps you understand the material. For remembering it, your time is better spent answering questions about the notes than copying them out again.

### What should I do with photos of slides from a lecture?

Treat them like any other page. Group them by lecture, turn them into questions, and check the answers against the slide deck your professor posted.
