You have a week and more than one final. Rereading every chapter won't fit, and it wouldn't work well even if it did. This plan turns your material into questions on day 1 and spends the rest of the week answering them on a schedule.

## Triage: rank your exams by weight times weakness

Before you study anything, spend 20 minutes deciding where your time goes. List every final and give each one two scores from 1 to 3.

- **Weight:** How much does this exam affect your grade? A final worth 40% of the course is a 3. A 15% final is a 1.
- **Weakness:** How shaky are you? Lost in half the units is a 3. Comfortable is a 1.

Multiply them. The highest number gets the most study blocks.

| Exam | Weight (1 to 3) | Weakness (1 to 3) | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Organic chemistry | 3 | 3 | 9 |
| Statistics | 2 | 2 | 4 |
| History | 2 | 1 | 2 |
| Spanish | 1 | 2 | 2 |

Also note the exam dates. An exam on day 3 of finals week needs its practice test earlier than one on day 7. Shift the plan below to fit.

## Day 1: turn every unit into questions

Day 1 is the setup day, and it's the most important one. Your goal is a question bank for every exam, not reviewing.

For each unit of each course:

1. Pull up the syllabus, lecture slides, study guide, and old quizzes.
2. Write 10 to 20 questions you could be asked. Mix short-answer and multiple-choice.
3. Write the answer on the back, or below, in your own words.
4. Mark units you couldn't write questions for. Those are your real gaps.

That last step matters. If you can't write a question about a topic, you can't answer one either. Now you know where to focus.

Doing this by hand for four courses takes most of the day. If you'd rather spend that time answering questions, [BrainLift](/auth/register) can build quizzes and flashcards from photos of your notes, your slides as a PDF, or typed notes. Skim every generated question against your notes, because AI can misread a slide or get a detail wrong. Delete anything off-topic.

For more on this step, see [how to turn a textbook or PDF into practice questions](/blog/textbook-pdf-to-practice-questions/).

## Days 2 to 5: interleave subjects, spaced reviews

These four days are where the learning happens. Each day, you answer questions from several courses, not one.

Split each day into blocks of 45 to 60 minutes with short breaks. Rotate subjects between blocks. Give your top-priority exam two or three blocks per day and lower ones one block.

| Block | Day 2 | Day 3 | Day 4 | Day 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Orgo, units 1 to 3 | Stats, units 1 to 2 | Orgo, units 4 to 6 | Stats, all units |
| 2 | Stats, units 1 to 2 | Orgo, units 4 to 6 | History, all units | Orgo, all missed cards |
| 3 | History, units 1 to 2 | Spanish, vocab | Orgo, units 1 to 3 again | Spanish, grammar |
| 4 | Orgo, missed cards | History, units 3 to 4 | Stats, units 3 to 4 | History, missed cards |
| Evening | Quick review of today's misses | Quick review of today's misses | Quick review of today's misses | Quick review of today's misses |

Notice units come back. Orgo units 1 to 3 show up on day 2 and again on day 4. That gap is the point. Reviews spread over time beat the same hours packed together, and in a review of hundreds of experiments, the best gap grew longer as the time until the test grew ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). With a few days until your exam, a gap of a day or two works well.

Rules for every block:

- **Answer first, then check.** Don't peek. Struggling to recall is the workout.
- **Sort the deck.** Put cards you got right in one pile and misses in another.
- **Missed cards come back sooner.** Review them in the evening and again the next day.
- **Explain, don't recite.** For concept-heavy classes, answer out loud as if teaching someone.

## Day 6: full practice test plus error log

Take one full practice exam for each subject that has one available. Use an old exam, a professor's practice test, or a mixed quiz from your question bank. Time it.

Then review every miss. Sort each one: did you not know it, misread it, make a careless slip, or run out of time? Only "didn't know it" needs a new flashcard. Misreads and timing problems need a habit fix, like circling "NOT" and "except" in the question.

For the full method, read [how to learn from your mistakes on practice tests](/blog/learn-from-practice-test-mistakes/).

Spend the rest of day 6 on the new cards from your misses.

## Day 7: light review and sleep

The day before an exam is not the day to learn new material. Keep it light.

- Run through your missed-cards pile once.
- Skim your formula sheet or key dates.
- Stop by early evening.

### What to do the night before

- **Pack everything.** ID, calculator, pencils, water, snack.
- **Do one short review.** 20 to 30 minutes on your hardest cards, then stop.
- **Skip the all-nighter.** Sleep is when your brain consolidates what you studied this week. Losing it trades lasting memory for a few extra pages you won't remember.
- **Set two alarms.** Seriously.

If you have back-to-back exams, run this day-7 routine the evening before each one.

## Why cramming feels better than it works

Cramming has a convincing feeling. After three hours on one chapter, it all seems familiar. That familiarity fools you.

In one study of students learning vocabulary with flashcards, spacing beat cramming for most learners. Yet many participants believed cramming had worked better ([Kornell, 2009](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994055985152)). Familiarity in the moment is not the same as being able to recall something days later.

That's why this plan feels harder than rereading. You'll miss questions on days 2 to 5. That's the plan working, not failing.

## The plan on one page

Copy this into your notes app or print it.

- **Before day 1 (20 min):** List exams. Score weight and weakness. Rank.
- **Day 1:** Write questions for every unit. Mark units you can't write questions for.
- **Days 2 to 5:** 45 to 60 minute blocks. Rotate subjects. Top priority gets the most blocks. Missed cards come back in the evening and the next day.
- **Day 6:** Timed practice test per subject. Review every miss. Make cards for knowledge gaps.
- **Day 7:** Light review of misses. Pack. Short review at night. Sleep.

Starting with more than a week? Spread days 2 to 5 over more days and add a second practice test. Younger students juggling classes and activities can find more ideas on our [high school study page](/for/high-school/).

## FAQ

### Can I really study for finals in a week?

You can make a lot of progress in a week if you spend it answering questions instead of rereading. You won't learn a semester from zero, so triage first and put your time where it moves your grade most.

### How many hours a day should I study during finals week?

Most students can manage six to eight focused hours a day across blocks with breaks. Past that, quality drops, so protect sleep and meals.

### Is it better to study one subject a day or mix them?

Mix them. Rotating subjects builds in spacing between reviews of each one, which helps retention more than spending a whole day on a single course.

### What if I have two finals on the same day?

Give both exams their practice test on day 5 or 6, and split day 7 between them. Focus the night-before review on the subject with the higher priority score.

### Should I make flashcards or practice questions?

Both work, since both make you recall the answer. Use flashcards for terms and facts, and practice questions for problems and applying concepts.
